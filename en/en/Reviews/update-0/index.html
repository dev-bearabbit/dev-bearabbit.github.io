Changing Jobs in the Age of AI

A reflection on career and change as a data engineer with six years of experience, navigating the age of AI.

To Begin With

Lately, I’ve been hearing more and more developers say they want to leave the profession. Looking around, it also seems like more people are actually leaving development or considering entirely different paths. And perhaps that isn’t so surprising.

AI can write code, find bugs, and even think through how to solve problems with us. Things that, only a few years ago, we assumed developers had to do themselves are now being handled by AI with remarkable ease. Thrown into this rapidly changing world, I couldn’t help but think about my own future as well.

What kind of work do I want to do from here? What kind of career do I want to build?

And this year, after thinking through those questions, I decided to leave the company I had been with for quite some time and move into a new environment.

Why I’m Still a Develope

When I first heard some of my colleagues say they were afraid of how quickly AI was advancing, I was a little surprised.

I had been thinking almost the opposite.

Isn’t AI allowing me to focus more on what development is really about?

To me, being a developer has never simply meant writing code or knowing how to use a particular technology. I think a developer is someone who finds problems in the world and uses technology to solve them.

From that perspective, AI was a fascinating tool.

In the past, solving a single problem meant searching for different approaches myself and testing them one by one. If it involved an unfamiliar field, I first had to study it from scratch, and even getting an idea to the point where I could actually build and test it often took a significant amount of time.

But with AI, that process became unbelievably fast.

I can ask about things I don’t know, get suggestions for approaches I hadn’t considered, and immediately start testing the next idea. Sometimes it feels as though I suddenly have access to a kind of collective intelligence — accumulated knowledge that I can question at any time.

As a result, I started attempting problems that I probably wouldn’t have touched before.

And I found that incredibly fun. After solving one problem, I would notice another interesting one. Once I solved that, I wanted to try something else. Over time, my project history as a data engineer became increasingly difficult to categorize. I would start with a data platform, wander into infrastructure, move from search to recommendation systems, and somehow eventually find myself building a product.

I had become a true jack-of-all-trades. Looking back, though, I think that process gave me quite a dopamine rush.

Finding a problem and actually solving it with technology and whatever new tools are available.

That, ultimately, was the part of being a developer that I loved. So when other people looked at AI and worried about the future of developers, AI felt a little different to me. Rather than something that takes away the essence of development, it felt more like a tool that allows me to take on a much wider range of problems.

Then Why Change Jobs?

If I had no intention of leaving development because of AI, why change companies at all?
Looking back, I think there were four main reasons.

1. From Platform to Business

Throughout my career as a data engineer, I spent a lot of time building and improving data platforms and pipelines. At first, I simply enjoyed making the platform itself better. I learned a lot from processing data more reliably, designing more efficient architectures, and improving existing systems from a technical perspective.

But as I gained more experience, my interests gradually began to shift.

Beyond building a good platform, I became increasingly interested in how the data was actually used by the business and what outcomes it ultimately produced.

For the next stage of my career, I wanted to move one step closer to the business itself.

2. Falling Into a Rut

To be a little candid, the work simply wasn’t as exciting to me as it used to be.

While we were building and improving the platform, there were always plenty of problems to solve. We built new things, changed existing architectures, and constantly encountered new challenges.

But once much of the platform work had matured, things naturally began to change.

Stable operations were obviously a good thing, but for me personally, there were fewer opportunities to encounter completely new problems. At some point, I began to feel like I was falling into a rut.

Rather than continuing to solve familiar problems well in a familiar environment, I wanted to put myself somewhere that would challenge me again.

3. A New Challenge

When you spend a long time in one environment, you naturally become accustomed to the way things work there. The technologies become familiar. You know where to look when something goes wrong. You develop an instinct for how certain problems should be solved.

But the more comfortable I became, the more curious I grew about other environments.

What tools do engineers use at other companies?
How do other engineers solve the same kinds of problems?
How would I work in a completely different environment?

I wanted to learn again — to work with different tools and see how other engineers approach and solve problems.

4. Suddenly, Six Years

Six years is a strange point in a career. I’m obviously no longer a junior, but it would still feel presumptuous to say that I know everything about any one field. At the same time, there are still plenty of directions I could choose from.

What kind of expertise do I want to develop as an engineer?

So far, I’ve mainly worked around platforms while dipping my toes into all kinds of different problems. This time, I want to experience problems that are more closely tied to the business.

Once I’ve had enough experience on both sides, I think I’ll have a clearer idea of which problems I want to go deeper into — and, ultimately, what kind of senior engineer I want to become.

Every Cloud Has a Silver Lining

People often say that every crisis brings an opportunity. It’s a cliché, but I can’t remember another time when it has felt quite this relevant.

I don’t intend to deny that the age of AI has brought real challenges. Concerns about fewer entry points for junior developers or declining demand for certain roles aren’t entirely unfounded.

But I also think AI has created just as many opportunities to try things that were previously out of reach. There has never been a time when a single person could attempt such a wide range of things alone.

So I’ve decided to keep being a developer.

I don’t think being a developer simply means writing code or knowing how to use a particular set of tools. In the end, aren’t developers people who build things that make our lives, our society, and our world at least a little better?

Languages and frameworks will keep changing. Even the tools I’m good at today may no longer exist a few years from now.

That’s why I want to build a career that isn’t defined by any particular tool.

A career defined not by what I built things with, but by the problems I solved.
